Data Preprocessing & Exploratory Audit

Prior to model training, the raw German Credit dataset was audited across four methodological dimensions:

  • Handling Missing Values: The dataset contains 1,000 borrower records across 20 input attributes. A programmatic scan verified exactly 0 missing, NaN, or corrupted values, consistent with the original documentation by Prof. Hofmann.
  • Categorical Feature Encoding: Thirteen attributes represent qualitative classifications (e.g., checking status, credit history, loan purpose, savings, employment duration, marital status/sex, housing). To avert the dummy variable trap and perfect multicollinearity in OLS and unpenalized logistic estimation, we implemented One-Hot Encoding dropping the first category (drop_first=True), yielding 41 indicator columns and 7 continuous regressors (48 total modeled predictors).
  • Feature Scaling: Continuous regressors (duration, credit_amount, installment_commitment, residence_since, age, existing_credits, num_dependents) exhibit wide variance in scale (e.g., loan durations reaching 72 months and credit obligations exceeding 15,000 DM). We fit a StandardScaler exclusively on the 70% training split (N=700, random_state=154) and transformed the 30% test split (N=300), strictly preventing data leakage.
  • Outlier Treatment Rationale: Using the standard Interquartile Range criterion (1.5 × IQR), we detected 70 outliers in duration (7.0%), 72 in credit_amount (7.2%), and 23 in age (2.3%). Crucially, these extreme observations were deliberately preserved without clipping or trimming. In credit risk underwriting, extreme borrowing amounts and extended durations represent legitimate tail-risk exposures; removing them sanitizes real-world vulnerability and blinds models to catastrophic default risks.

Question 2: Simpson's Paradox and Subgroup Heterogeneity ~290 words

A rigorous fairness audit requires identifying subgroups where aggregate relationships break down or invert. In our analysis of the German Credit dataset, we uncovered a dramatic instance of Simpson’s Paradox when evaluating the effect of existing bank credit lines (existing_credits) across demographic age cohorts: Young Adults (<30 years, N=371) versus Mature Adults (≥30 years, N=629).

In the aggregate population model (N=1,000), the bivariate logistic regression coefficient for existing_credits is positive: βFull = +0.1780 (SE = 0.1232, p = 0.1488, 95% CI: [-0.0636, +0.4195]). Superficially, an underwriting policy based on the full population might conclude that having additional existing credits is a neutral-to-positive indicator of borrower reliability. However, disaggregating by age exposes an acute sign reversal: for Young Adults (<30 years), βYoung = -0.2203 (SE = 0.2133, p = 0.3016, 95% CI: [-0.6384, +0.1977]), whereas for Mature Adults (≥30 years), βMature = +0.3083 (SE = 0.1586, p = 0.0519, 95% CI: [-0.0025, +0.6192]).

The coefficient reverses from negative (-0.2203) for young applicants to strongly positive (+0.3083) for older applicants, where it achieves marginal statistical significance (p = 0.0519). Statistically and economically, this reversal reflects distinct latent behavioral meanings across life stages. For mature adults, maintaining multiple existing credit accounts reflects an established credit history, long-standing banking relationships, and proven repayment discipline over time. For young adults under 30, however, possessing multiple credit lines typically indicates financial instability, over-leveraging, and emergency liquidity distress, as young borrowers have not had sufficient years to build seasoned, amortized credit lines.

A parallel subgroup divergence emerges when examining age across gender in a standardized multivariable model. While the aggregate age coefficient is positive (β = +0.2260, p = 0.0036), it is statistically meaningless for men (βMale = +0.0604, p = 0.5204), yet acts as an enormous protective factor for women (βFemale = +0.4741, p = 0.0013), where the effect size is nearly eight-fold larger.

Policy Implication: If policymakers deploy an aggregate scoring algorithm that ignores subgroup interactions, the policy will inadvertently reward over-indebted young borrowers who take on additional loans while simultaneously mispricing young female applicants whose financial trajectories differ radically from male baselines. Subgroup disaggregation is mandatory to prevent aggregate models from acting as discriminatory blunt instruments.

Question 3: Omitted-Variable Bias (OVB) Stress-Testing ~285 words

To empirically evaluate the vulnerability of credit scoring to unobserved confounders, we deliberately omitted checking_status (Attribute 1), the single most influential determinant of short-term liquid cash reserves. We trained a Full Logistic Regression model containing all 48 preprocessed features on the training set (N=700) and compared its coefficients against a Reduced Logistic Regression model where all dummy indicators for checking account status (checking_status_A12, A13, A14) were purged.

The shifts in key predictors reveal massive coefficient instability: savings_status_A63 (moderate savings 500–1000 DM) shifted from +0.3720 in the full model to +0.7812 in the reduced model—an astonishing +110.00% surge (Δβ = +0.4092). employment_A73 (employment tenure 1–4 years) experienced an almost identical doubling, shifting from +0.1278 to +0.2560 (+100.31% increase). credit_history_A33 (past payment delays) inflated from +0.3941 to +0.5956 (+51.14% change), and existing_credits shifted from -0.0983 to -0.0656 (+33.31% change). Applicant age shifted from +0.1543 to +0.1869 (+21.16%), while standardized loan duration remained largely invariant, moving from -0.4273 to -0.4325 (-1.21%).

The predictor that shifted the most was savings_status_A63 (+110.00%). This massive inflation exposes the statistical mechanics of omitted-variable bias: when a dominant explanatory variable (Z) that is correlated with both loan outcome (Y) and other covariates (X) is excluded, the remaining regressors absorb Z's omitted explanatory power through proxy correlation: E[beta_X_hat] = beta_X + beta_Z * [Cov(X, Z) / Var(X)]. Because cash liquidity in a checking account is positively correlated with accumulated savings and stable employment tenure, purging checking status forced the regression optimizer to artificially inflate the weights on savings accounts and employment to compensate for the missing liquidity signal.

This finding demonstrates that an auditor cannot trust the weights of an observational model without rigorous confounding stress-tests. A practitioner inspecting the reduced model would falsely conclude that moderate savings accounts exert more than double their true structural protective influence. The model appears statistically sound, yet its internal weights are corrupted by proxy absorption, rendering adverse action notices and credit explanations legally vulnerable and misleading.

Question 4: Detecting Hidden Confounds in Production Systems ~295 words

In enterprise credit scoring, detecting unobservable confounders—such as informal family wealth, unrecorded remittances, or private guarantees—cannot be accomplished through standard internal validation metrics (e.g., cross-validated AUC or residual deviance), because an omitted confounder biases both training and validation splits identically. To rigorously detect and bound hidden confounds in production, I propose a dual-track strategy combining Quasi-Experimental Instrumental Variable (IV) Estimation with Oster-Rosenbaum Sensitivity Bounding.

1. Quasi-Experimental Instrumental Variable (Two-Stage Predictor Residualization): We identify an exogenous instrument (Z) that affects an applicant’s financial observables but is uncorrelated with their latent creditworthiness or unobserved family wealth. In retail banking, an ideal instrument is hyper-local banking competition shocks—for instance, the staggered municipal roll-out of regional credit union branches or postal-code-level changes in banking service fees. Because these geographical regulatory shifts affect checking account adoption and loan duration without altering an individual's personal moral hazard or innate repayment propensity, they satisfy the instrument relevance (Cov(Z, X) ≠ 0) and exclusion restrictions (Cov(Z, ε) = 0). By running a two-stage least squares / control function regression: Stage 1 estimates X_hat from Z and covariates; Stage 2 estimates default from X_hat and covariates. A Hausman specification test comparing the baseline model's coefficient beta_OLS_hat against beta_IV_hat formally tests for confounding. A statistically significant divergence (p < 0.05) mathematically proves the presence of an omitted confounder corrupting the observational weights.

2. Oster Bounding and Negative Controls: Because perfect instruments can be difficult to secure, we complement the IV audit with Oster's coefficient stability methodology. By evaluating how coefficients move relative to changes in R2 as observable controls are incrementally added, we compute Oster's proportionality metric δ: the degree of selection on unobservables relative to observables required to explain away the observed effect. If δ < 1.0, the model is deemed fragile to hidden confounding. Finally, we introduce a negative control outcome—such as applicant telephone registration status—which should have zero theoretical causal connection to credit default. If the model assigns significant predictive weight to the negative control, hidden socio-economic confounding is actively contaminating the feature space.

Question 6: The Real-World Cost of Fairness Trade-Offs ~290 words

To understand what fairness metrics mean for real human beings, we examine how decision thresholds on our parametric trade-off curve affect individual applicants across sex demographics: Group A (Female, N=85, base approval rate 69.41%) and Group B (Male, N=215, base approval rate 71.16%).

Consider two realistic applicants in our test set: Elena (Female, 28 years old, employed 2 years, renting, seeking a 2,500 DM catering equipment loan, predicted approval P_hat = 0.480) and Marcus (Male, 29 years old, employed 2 years, renting, seeking an identical 2,500 DM auto repair loan, predicted approval P_hat = 0.480).

Now observe their life outcomes under two contrasting operational thresholds: under an Equalized Odds / Inclusion Threshold (t = 0.35), both Elena and Marcus exceed the cutoff (0.480 ≥ 0.35). Both are approved for credit; Elena launches her catering enterprise and Marcus repairs his vehicle. At this operating point, Demographic Parity is high (DP = 0.9292), Female approval rate is 84.71%, Male approval rate is 91.16%, and Equalized Odds is robust (EO = 0.8108), with true positive rates virtually equalized (TPRFemale = 93.22% vs TPRMale = 95.42%).

However, if a bank risk committee shifts to an Accuracy-Maximizing Threshold (t = 0.55) to achieve peak accuracy (76.33%), both Elena and Marcus are summarily denied credit (0.480 < 0.55). Furthermore, this shift disproportionately harms women: female approval plummets from 84.71% to 68.24% (a 16.47 percentage point drop), dragging Demographic Parity down to 0.8480. Equalized Odds collapses to 0.5156, because female False Positive Rates drop to 30.77% while male False Positive Rates remain high at 59.68%. Qualified women are excluded at far higher rates relative to men.

The Inherent Trade-Off Dilemma: If a regulator demands perfect Demographic Parity (DP = 1.000) at t=0.55, the bank would be forced to apply disparate thresholds: setting the female threshold at t=0.45 (approving Elena) while maintaining the male threshold at t=0.55 (denying Marcus). Under this regime, Marcus is denied credit while Elena is approved, despite possessing the identical computed risk score of 0.480. Prioritizing group demographic parity directly violates individual fairness and predictive parity. Conversely, enforcing an identical threshold sacrifices Equalized Odds and locks historically under-represented women into depressed loan approval rates.

Question 8: Strategic Policy Choice: Demographic Parity vs. Equalized Odds ~290 words

If forced by a banking regulatory authority to choose between prioritizing Demographic Parity (DP) or Equalized Odds (EO) in retail credit underwriting, I unequivocally recommend prioritizing Equalized Odds (EO).

Justification Grounded in Empirical Data: In our dataset, creditworthiness is not distributed identically across historical records due to legacy systemic inequities: baseline good credit rates stand at 69.41% for women (59/85) and 71.16% for men (153/215). Under Kleinberg’s Impossibility Theorem, when base rates differ between demographic groups (Y ¬⊥ A), Demographic Parity and Equalized Odds are mathematically incompatible with Predictive Parity.

Prioritizing Equalized Odds aligns lending policy with the ethical imperative of meritocratic fairness: individuals who are genuinely capable of repaying their debts should face the exact same probability of loan approval regardless of their sex. At our recommended threshold of t = 0.35, Equalized Opportunity is maximized: creditworthy females achieve a TPR of 93.22%, virtually matching creditworthy males at 95.42% (a minuscule 2.20% gap, ratio = 0.9769). Furthermore, Predictive Parity is preserved (PP = 1.0255), ensuring that loan approval signals identical repayment probability (76.39% for females vs 74.49% for males).

The Sacrificed Metric and Why It Is Acceptable: Under this policy, we explicitly sacrifice strict Demographic Parity. At t = 0.35, DP is 0.9292 (a 7.08% shortfall from parity), and at the default threshold of t = 0.55, DP drops to 0.8480 (a 15.20% shortfall).

Sacrificing pure Demographic Parity is not only acceptable but ethically necessary in consumer credit. Enforcing mathematical parity in acceptance rates (P(Y_hat=1|Female) = P(Y_hat=1|Male)) when underlying income and savings distributions differ would require the financial institution to either: (1) artificially approve un-creditworthy female applicants simply to fulfill a numerical quota, pushing financially vulnerable women into default, asset repossession, and ruined credit scores; or (2) arbitrarily deny creditworthy male applicants who meet all objective underwriting criteria solely because the male quota has been exhausted. Imposing quotas under the guise of Demographic Parity harms the very individuals it purports to protect by saddling them with unmanageable debt burdens. Equalized Odds protects qualified applicants from demographic discrimination while preserving fiscal solvency and institutional trust.

The fundamental lesson of this audit is that summarizing a sociotechnical AI system into a single performance statistic—whether overall accuracy (75.33%), AUC-ROC (0.792), or an aggregate F1-score—is an act of intellectual complacency that masks dangerous structural trade-offs.

First, aggregate accuracy systematically masks catastrophic subgroup performance collapses. At our model’s peak accuracy operating threshold of t = 0.55 (76.33% overall accuracy), Equalized Odds collapses to a dismal 0.5156, with the model approving non-creditworthy men at nearly double the rate of non-creditworthy women (FPRMale = 59.68% vs FPRFemale = 30.77%), while female loan approvals plunge to 68.24%. A policymaker celebrating "76% accuracy" is completely blind to the fact that half of the equalized odds metric has been destroyed.

Second, a single threshold is an unstated value judgment, not a mathematical fact. Choosing between t = 0.35 (prioritizing inclusion and Equalized Odds of 0.8108) and t = 0.55 (prioritizing bank profitability and default suppression) cannot be resolved by data science. It is a political choice trading off borrower economic access against institutional capital loss. Reducing this to an automated "deploy" decision abdicates societal responsibility to an arbitrary mathematical constant (0.50).

Third, fairness metrics are mathematically adversarial. As proved by Kleinberg and Chouldechova, when baseline default rates differ (69.41% female vs 71.16% male), Demographic Parity, Equalized Odds, and Predictive Parity cannot simultaneously equal 1.0. Improving DP from 0.7547 to 0.7869 (+4.27%) causes Equalized Odds to plunge from 0.3974 to 0.2981 (-24.99%)—a punishing trade-off cost ratio of 5.85. Any single index obscures which community is bearing the cost of that trade-off.

Our model confidently misclassified 24 test cases (8.00% of the cohort), projecting P_hat ≥ 0.75 on defaulting borrowers simply because they were homeowners (83.33%) with moderate checking balances (45.83%), oblivious to their overwhelming credit burden (3,337.50 DM).
